80% of contractor leads need follow-up. Most never get it.

The deals you think you lost mostly aren't lost. They're sitting in a text thread nobody picked back up.

Contractors are unusually good at the hard part. You can diagnose a system nobody else could figure out, price it honestly, and do work that lasts fifteen years. Then a homeowner asks for a quote, you send it Thursday night from the truck, and you never hear back — and that's where it ends. Not because you're bad at sales. Because following up Friday, then Monday, then the week after requires sitting at a desk thinking about it, and you were under a house.

The numbers on this are brutal and well documented. Research compiled by Marketing Donut found that 80% of sales require five or more follow-ups after the initial contact, while 44% of salespeople give up after just one. And that 44% describes people whose entire job is selling — a contractor doing quotes between service calls is working with a fraction of that attention.

Why contractors lose leads to silence, not rejection

Go back through last quarter's quotes and count how many customers actually told you no. For most shops: almost none. People rarely reject a contractor out loud — they just stop replying, and the thread ends mid-sentence.

We read that silence as a decision. It usually isn't one. The homeowner got pulled into something at work. They're waiting on their spouse to look at the number. The adjuster hasn't called back. They meant to reply, felt bad that four days had passed, and then replying got awkward — so they didn't. None of that is about you, your price, or your reviews. It's a conversation that ran out of momentum.

Which explains the thing that sounds like a sales cliché until you see the mechanism: the contractor who follows up a fourth or fifth time often wins the job. Not because persistence wore anyone down. Because on the fourth message the timing finally lined up — the spouse agreed, the check cleared, the unit started making the noise again. Follow-up isn't persuasion. It's showing up again at a moment that works. You can't know which moment that is, so you cover several.

A lead that never said no isn't a lost lead. It's an unfinished conversation — and unfinished conversations are the cheapest revenue in your business, because you already paid to start them.

What good follow-up actually looks like for a service business

"Just checking in" is the weakest message in the trades, and the one almost everybody sends. It contains no new information and quietly puts the work back on the customer — they have to remember the job, find the quote, and restart the thread themselves. Real follow-up carries its own weight.

  • It's timed, not random. The first 48 hours do most of the work, then the spacing stretches out — a couple of days, then a week, then longer. Five messages in five days is pestering. Five messages over a month is diligence.
  • It's specific. "Following up on the condenser at the Maple Street property" beats "checking in" every time — it hands the customer the context instead of asking for it.
  • It's multi-channel. Text gets opened within minutes and is right for nudges and scheduling. Email carries the quote, the photos, the financing options. Using only one of the two cuts your reach for no reason.
  • It adapts to temperature. Someone who replied twice and asked about pricing needs a different cadence than someone who filled out a form and vanished. Treating both the same wastes one and annoys the other.
  • It ends cleanly. Every lead should reach a definite state — booked, or a real no. A pile of maybes is worse than a short list of nos — they keep occupying the part of your brain that should be on the next job.

Automated vs. manual follow-up

The usual argument for manual follow-up is that it's more personal. In practice the comparison isn't personal-versus-automated. It's automated versus nothing, because manual follow-up doesn't survive contact with a real week.

And it fails in the worst possible pattern. Follow-up is the first thing dropped when you get busy, because nobody is standing in your kitchen waiting for it — the customer who didn't reply doesn't complain about being forgotten. So it breaks down precisely during your busiest weeks, which are also the weeks with the most leads, the highest urgency, and the biggest tickets. Manual follow-up is strongest in February and gone in July.

Automation isn't better because it's smarter. It's better because it's indifferent — it sends the day-9 message on day 9 whether the week went well or not, at the same quality as day 1, across every lead at once. That consistency is the entire advantage.

Worth being honest about the failure mode, though: automation done badly is worse than none. Generic blasts that ignore what the customer asked about, or sequences that keep firing after someone replied, cost you goodwill. It hinges on messages written for your trade and a system that shuts up the second a human enters the conversation.

Questions contractors ask about follow-up

How many times should you follow up with a lead?
At least five times, and more if the job is large. Research compiled by Marketing Donut found that 80% of sales require five or more follow-ups after the first contact, while 44% of salespeople stop after a single attempt. That gap is where most contractor revenue quietly disappears — not in leads that said no, but in leads that were never contacted a second time.
Isn't following up five times annoying?
It is when every message says the same thing. Five copies of "just checking in" reads as pestering because it asks the customer to do the work of restarting the conversation. Follow-up that references their actual job, spaces itself out sensibly, and gives them an easy way to book or decline reads as professional. In practice, homeowners more often apologize for not replying sooner than complain about being contacted.
How long should you keep following up before giving up?
Until the lead books or gives you a clear no. Most quotes that go quiet aren't rejections — the customer is waiting on a spouse, an insurance adjuster, a payday, or the next time the problem gets bad enough. A lead that never answered either way should stay in a long, low-frequency sequence rather than being deleted. Contractors routinely book work from leads that sat silent for 30 to 90 days.
Can automated follow-up be done without sounding like a robot?
Yes, and the difference comes down to two things: whether the messages were written for your trade and your voice, and whether the sequence stops the moment a real person replies. Generic mass-blasted templates are what make automation obvious. Messages that mention the customer's actual situation, arrive at sensible hours, and hand off to a human at the right moment read exactly like a business that stays on top of its work.
