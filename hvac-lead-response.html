Why HVAC companies lose jobs to slow callbacks — and how to fix it

The lead didn't go cold because your price was too high or your reviews weren't good enough. It went cold because somebody else picked up first.

It's 2:40 on an August afternoon and you're in an attic with a meter in your hand. The phone buzzes, you can't get to it, and it rolls to voicemail. Forty minutes later you're back in the truck — and by then that homeowner has filled out the form on two other websites and one of those companies has already texted her back. She wasn't weighing three quotes. She booked the first shop that acted like it wanted the work.

That's the whole loss, and it happened before anyone said a word about price. Research from Drift, which audited response times across hundreds of companies, puts the average business at about 47 hours to reply to a new inbound lead — while buyer studies consistently find roughly 78% of customers hire whoever responds first. Those two numbers next to each other explain more lost HVAC revenue than any marketing problem does.

What happens to an HVAC lead in the first 5 minutes

The most useful research here is a study led by Dr. James Oldroyd at MIT, published in Harvard Business Review as "The Short Life of Online Sales Leads." It tracked thousands of inbound leads against how quickly someone responded. The finding that matters for you: contacting a lead within five minutes made a company 21 times more likely to qualify that lead than contacting it at the 30-minute mark. Not 21% better. Twenty-one times.

The part people miss is the shape of the curve. It isn't a gentle slope where an hour is a little worse than a minute — it's a cliff. Nearly all of the drop happens in the first half hour, and after that the odds flatten out near the bottom and stay there. Which is why "I called them back that evening" and "I called them back three days later" produce almost the same result. Once you're measuring in hours, you already lost the part of the race that counted.

For HVAC, compress that window further. A homeowner with no cooling in July isn't shopping — they're triaging. They're in a house getting worse by the hour with a phone in their hand, and they'll keep contacting companies until one answers. The question in their head isn't "who's the best value," it's "who can actually come out." First real human response wins, and it usually isn't close.

The cruelest detail: the calls most likely to be lost to a slow callback are the same ones most likely to become a full system replacement.

The real cost of a missed HVAC lead

Run the arithmetic and it stops being abstract. A typical repair ticket lands around $300 to $500; a system replacement runs $8,000 to $15,000. Now assume you miss three or four emergency leads a month — not aggressive for a shop where the owner is also running calls in peak season. At repair value alone that's $1,200 to $2,000 a month walking to a competitor: fifteen to twenty-four thousand a year, from the bottom of the range.

And that's the floor, because it assumes every one of those calls was a small repair. Statistically some weren't — one 14-year-old unit that finally quit in a heat wave doubles the number on its own.

Here's what makes it worse than an ordinary lost sale: you already paid for those leads. Between Google Local Services, search ads, and Angi, you spent somewhere between $75 and $250 to get each of those phone numbers into your hands, and you pay whether you answer or not. A missed lead isn't a lead you failed to win — it's a lead you bought and handed to the shop down the road, along with whatever that customer is worth over the next ten years of maintenance and the eventual replacement.

Most owners in this spot conclude they need more leads. Usually they need to stop leaking the ones they're already paying for. It's a cheaper problem and a faster fix.

How a done-for-you lead response service works

The premise is simple: the gap between "lead arrives" and "appointment on the calendar" stops being your job. Here's what runs in that gap.

  • Instant response, by text and email. Every new lead gets a reply in under 60 seconds in your business's name — 11pm Sunday the same as 11am Tuesday. That reply is the one that takes the customer off the market.
  • A short qualification exchange. What's the system doing, how old is it, is it out completely or running badly, where's the property. Enough to know what you'd be walking into — not a twelve-question interrogation that makes people quit.
  • Urgency ranking. A no-heat call in January and a "thinking about replacing this in the spring" inquiry are not the same lead and shouldn't sit in the same undifferentiated list. Ready-to-buy work sorts to the top.
  • Follow-up that doesn't quit. Leads who go quiet get worked on a real schedule across text and email until they book or tell you no. This is where most of the recoverable money is.
  • Booking straight onto your calendar. Qualified customers get scheduled. You see an appointment with a warm homeowner who already knows who you are.

The distinction worth holding onto: you don't operate any of this. No dashboard to check, no workflow to maintain, no login for your techs. It runs whether you think about it or not, which is the only version that survives a busy season — and it's the whole premise behind how Amendr works.

Is this different from GoHighLevel or ServiceTitan?

Yes — and worth being direct about, because both are good products doing different jobs.

Software

GoHighLevel, HubSpot, Zapier

Toolkits. They give you the ability to send an instant text — after somebody builds the workflow, writes the messages, connects the lead sources, and maintains it. That somebody is usually you, at night, after a full day of calls.

Operations

ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro

Excellent at everything downstream of a booked job — dispatch, scheduling, invoicing, payroll. They assume the appointment already exists; the lead that never got answered never enters the system.

Self-configured tools also share a failure mode nobody warns you about: they break quietly. A form field gets renamed, an integration expires, a sequence stops firing — and because nothing errors out loudly, you find out three weeks and forty leads later.

A managed service is accountable for the outcome instead of the tooling. What gets measured isn't whether an automation is switched on — it's response time, follow-up completion, and appointments booked. A lead sitting unanswered is a failure on our side of the line, not a setting you forgot to enable.

Questions HVAC owners ask

How fast should an HVAC company respond to a new lead?
Inside five minutes, and faster if the call is an emergency. MIT research by Dr. James Oldroyd, published in Harvard Business Review, found that responding within five minutes made a lead 21 times more likely to be qualified than responding at the 30-minute mark. For a homeowner with no cooling in July, the practical window is shorter still — they're filling out two or three forms in a row, and the first company that answers usually gets the job.
What is an HVAC lead response service?
It's a service that takes over what happens between a lead arriving and an appointment being booked. Every new inquiry gets an immediate reply by text and email, a short set of qualifying questions, a spot in an urgency-ranked queue, and follow-up that continues until the customer books or says no. Unlike software, you're not the one operating it — the response happens whether you're on a roof, on a ladder, or asleep.
Does response speed really matter for emergency HVAC calls?
It matters more there than anywhere else. A homeowner with a dead system in a heat wave isn't comparison shopping — they're triaging. They'll take the first company that confirms someone can actually come out. Those are also the highest-value calls on your board, because a failed system in peak season is the one most likely to turn into a full replacement rather than a repair.
Do I have to change my dispatch software or CRM?
No. Amendr sits in front of whatever you already run — ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Jobber, a paper schedule on the wall. It handles the leg between the lead arriving and the appointment being set, then hands the booked job to your existing calendar. Nothing gets ripped out and your techs don't learn a new system.
