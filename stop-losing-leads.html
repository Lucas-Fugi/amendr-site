Amendr › Where your leads are going

You're paying for leads. Here's where they're actually going.

The gap between what your lead count says you should be making and what you're actually making has a specific location. It's usually findable in about thirty minutes.

The ads are running. Google Local Services is charging you. Angi sends leads, the website form works, the referrals keep coming. You can scroll through your inbox and see them arriving. And yet the revenue at the end of the month doesn't look anything like what that volume should produce.

The reflex is to blame the leads. They're tire-kickers, they're price shoppers, Angi's selling the same name to four companies. Some of that is true — every business gets junk leads. But junk leads aren't what creates the gap you're feeling, because junk leads were never going to convert either way. The gap comes from the good leads that got handled badly. And that happens entirely after the lead arrives, in a stretch of the process most owners have never actually looked at.

The three places leads die

In practice, nearly all of it is one of three failures. They're not exotic, and none of them are about how good you are at the work.

1. Slow response

Research from Drift, which audited inbound response times across hundreds of companies, found the average business takes roughly 47 hours to reply to a new lead. Buyer studies consistently show about 78% of customers hire whoever gets back to them first. And the MIT study led by Dr. James Oldroyd, published in Harvard Business Review, found that replying within five minutes made a lead 21 times more likely to qualify than replying at the 30-minute mark. Put those together and the picture is unforgiving: by the time you return the call that evening, the decision was made hours ago by someone who answered faster. You never got to compete.

2. No follow-up

One call, a voicemail, and then nothing. Research compiled by Marketing Donut found that 80% of sales require five or more follow-ups, while 44% of salespeople give up after one. Almost none of your unconverted leads told you no — they just went quiet, and quiet got treated as an answer. Those are unfinished conversations sitting in a folder, and most of them are still winnable.

3. No prioritization

This one is the least discussed and often the most expensive. Leads arrive in the order they arrive, which is random with respect to how much they're worth. So you spend thirty minutes on the phone with someone comparing prices for a job they'll do next spring, and while you're on that call, a homeowner with a dead system goes to voicemail and calls the next company.

The cost isn't only the missed call. It's that your best available hour went to your worst available lead — and you had no way to know, because nothing in your inbox distinguishes an emergency from an idle question.

Three failures, one shared trait: every one of them happens to leads you already paid for.

How to diagnose your lead leak

You can do this yourself this week, without buying anything. It takes about half an hour and it will tell you which of the three is actually costing you money — because guessing wrong here means fixing the wrong thing.

The thirty-minute audit

  • Get last month's leads into one list. Every source — web forms, LSA, Angi, call logs, the texts to your cell. Most owners have never seen all of them in one place, and the count alone is usually a surprise.
  • Measure real response time. For each lead, note when it came in and when your first outbound contact went out. Use the median, not the average — a few instant replies will paper over a lot of slow ones. Then look at your ten worst.
  • Count attempts per lead. Be strict about it: one call plus a voicemail is one attempt, not two. Most shops discover their average is somewhere between one and two.
  • Calculate your set rate. Leads in, appointments booked. Then appointments to sold jobs. If a lot of leads never become appointments but most appointments close, your problem is upstream of your sales ability entirely.
  • Mystery-shop yourself. Submit your own web form at 7pm on a Saturday from a number nobody at your company recognizes. Time how long until anything happens. This takes five minutes and is usually the most uncomfortable data point on the list.
  • Check the after-hours split. What percentage of last month's leads arrived outside of 8-to-5, Monday to Friday? For most home-service businesses it's close to half — and those are the ones sitting overnight.

Now read the results. Median response over an hour? Speed is your biggest lever, and nothing else you fix will matter as much. Under three attempts per lead? Follow-up is where your money is. Both healthy and the set rate is still bad? Then you genuinely do have a lead-quality problem — and the fix is changing where leads come from, not working the current ones harder. Either way you now know which conversation to have, which is more than most owners in your position have.

How to fix it — with or without us

There are three real options. They all work. They cost very different amounts and demand very different things from you.

Option 1

Hire a dedicated dispatcher

Roughly $3,000–$4,000 a month fully loaded. You get a real person with judgment who can handle a complicated call. You also get one shift of coverage, vacation weeks, turnover, and another employee to manage — and half your leads still arrive after they've gone home.

Option 2

Build it yourself

GoHighLevel or similar runs a modest monthly fee, which is the cheap part. The expensive part is weeks of configuration, writing every message, connecting every lead source, and owning it forever — including noticing the silent Tuesday when a sequence stops firing. Good fit if somebody on your team actually enjoys this work.

Option 3

A done-for-you service

Response, qualification, ranking, follow-up, and booking handled for you at a fraction of a dispatcher's cost, covering nights and weekends by default. The trade-off is honest: it isn't a person on the phone for a complex conversation. It gets the lead answered, sorted, worked, and booked — then hands you a warm customer. That's the job Amendr does.

Option 4

Change nothing, buy more leads

The default choice, and the only genuinely bad one. More volume through a leaking process means paying twice for the same problem — once for the lead, and again in the job that went to whoever answered first.

Whichever route you take, do the audit first. Half an hour with last month's leads will tell you more about your business than another month of ad spend, and it costs nothing but the willingness to look at the number.

Questions about losing leads

Why am I losing leads even though I'm getting plenty of them?
In most service businesses the loss happens after the lead arrives, not before. Three things account for nearly all of it: responding too slowly, following up once or not at all, and working leads in the order they came in instead of by urgency. Research from Drift puts the average business response time at about 47 hours, and buyer studies consistently show roughly 78% of customers hire whoever responds first — so a slow reply loses the job before quality, price, or reputation ever gets considered.
How do I find out my actual lead response time?
Take last month's leads from every source, put them in one spreadsheet, and for each one record the timestamp it arrived and the timestamp of your first outbound contact. Use the median rather than the average, because a handful of instant replies will hide a lot of slow ones. Then submit your own web form on a Saturday evening from a phone number nobody at your company recognizes and time how long until something happens. That single test tells most owners more than the spreadsheet does.
Is it lead quality or my follow-up process?
You can tell them apart with two numbers. If your median response time is over an hour, speed is your biggest problem. If your average number of contact attempts per lead is under three, follow-up is. Only when both of those are healthy and your set rate is still low do you have a genuine lead-quality issue — at which point the fix is changing where the leads come from, not working harder on the ones you get.
What does it cost to fix a lead leak?
There are three realistic routes. A dedicated dispatcher or in-house CSR runs roughly $3,000 to $4,000 a month fully loaded and covers one shift. Building it yourself in a platform like GoHighLevel costs a modest monthly software fee plus weeks of configuration and permanent ownership of maintenance. A done-for-you service handles response, follow-up, and booking for a fraction of a dispatcher's cost and covers nights and weekends. The most expensive option is the one most owners default to: leaving the leak in place and buying more leads to compensate.
